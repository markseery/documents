Intelligence and the Commonwealth: AI First Principles

# Preamble

The emergence of AI as a commonly accessed and used technology is an important moment in the history of human civilization, filled with human fear, greed, and opportunity. A moment where we consider first principles, what we hold to be self-evident, what we believe to be true, and what values are most dear. The public square must remain open, so all can express their views, without fear of recrimination from a vocal majority, leveraging the best ideas and aspirations.

The moment must be met with a balancing of competing interests, in the context of both contracted terms and the common wealth. Individuals, organizations, nations, and countries have different incentives, motivations, and values. These differences must be constructively navigated and constructively considered. As a general principle and proclivity, productive discussion of issues is more important than attacking others who are different. Differences emerge for many reasons, not all of them nefarious.

### A few principles stand out as being central:

* Human lives that are long and prosperous, with guardrails around threats, opportunity to prosper, and protection of rights.
* A right to use a technique, for example distillation, should be decided not as a blanket policy across all individuals and organizations, but based on the terms of service agreed to, by those using products, services, and technologies. Violating terms of service is often more the problem than a technique itself.
* Self-interest is an incentive and motivation to contribute to a wide scope of public and private sector activities. Self-interest is not an inherently bad force; it is often the engine of prosperity for individuals and groups. The inability to constructively balance competing interests is a significant challenge at a systems level.
* Organizations are useful to humans insofar as they contribute to long and prosperous lives. Frameworks place guardrails around organizations, and protect the rights of organizations, only insofar as human life is long and prosperous. This is true for public and private sector organizations.
* One expression of the prosperity is the embrace of different missions. Public sector, open systems, and proprietary systems have different missions, and can harm and enhance human life in different ways. Embracing different missions should be fundamental. Mission clarity should also be fundamental.
* A preference to leverage the legal system to resolve differences and clarify the rules of the road.

### How are mistakes corrected and wisdom preserved:

* The scientific method.
* Debate in an open public square.
* Elections.
* Tradition (precedent/stare decisis, culture, values).
* Legislation and enforcement.

### Key concerns:

* Fear overwhelms to the point that innovation, opportunity, and prosperity are constrained.
* Greed overwhelms to the point that the power to coerce makes life for humans more brutish and less prosperous. This has often manifested as a fear of public or private-sector concentration.
* Institutions, systems, and technology do not reflect our fundamental humanity, and that which is highly valued.
* Terms of contracts are not understood or enforced; they are relegated when a loud voice objects.
* Potential, destructive powers of any new technology, this time AI, are unconstrained, leading to human catastrophe.

No document can hope to consider all future questions that will become before legislators, courts, community leaders, or individuals. Worst case, there is good articulation of current top-of-mind issues. Best case there are communicated principles and values that are relevant for years to come.

This document will be placed in an accessible repository. All are invited to comment and/or fork.

# Intelligence and the Commonwealth: AI First Principles

## 1. Foundational Premise

Humans are born naked and free, subject to threats and opportunities. To ensure life is not brutish and short, societies construct guardrails against threats while expanding the range of opportunities. These threats and opportunities exist in physical and mental domains.

We direct our physical and mental potential according to multiple considerations. When that direction is based on explicitly stated or reasonably assumed liberties, rewards, protections, opportunities, and outcomes, those expectations deserve respect. Breaking them after the fact undermines the very mechanisms that drive contribution and progress—for example, open and proprietary terms.

This framework makes no claim that governments or organizations possess natural rights. The argument is narrower and more concrete: people contribute their physical and mental potential under the terms of a system or contract, and those terms must be respected. The relevant terms may be financial reward for the individual and other stakeholders, or free common use of the contribution within a system. In either case, the obligation is to the contract under which the contribution was made, not to any natural entitlement of the organization or the state. The entitlements of organizations and states exist insofar as they serve the lives of humans.

Open systems and closed systems typically operate under different mission and operating models. Therefore, they engage with different contracts and rewards. When individuals or organizations contribute physical or mental potential to a system, the realization of that potential should be rewarded in accordance with contracts. Part of the expansion of opportunity in a society should be the embrace of different missions.

* Contribution to an open system whose terms explicitly identify free use of the contribution for the benefit of the community should result in free use.
* Contribution to a closed system in which contributors are promised financial or other proprietary returns if they are generated should result in fidelity to the contract.
* When an open system requires those using the system to agree to usage terms, then those usage terms should be respected. For example, if distillation is explicitly allowed, that should be respected.
* When a closed system requires those using the system to agree to usage terms, then those usage terms should be respected. For example, if distillation is explicitly disallowed, that should be respected.

In other words, there should be no blanket law about a practice like distillation. The reference point should be the terms of use agreed to. If the terms of use are unlawful, then it is for the courts to clarify and correct. Open systems have the right to enforce their objective that the technology they have developed is open to all, if that is what community members agreed to. At the same time, stakeholders in a closed community should be protected from theft and uses that violate terms of use, because of the costs and liabilities they are open to.

It is not asserted that organizations whose model contemplates rewarding stakeholders with profit have a right to profit. Most stakeholders who contribute to closed systems do so with the understanding that profitable buyers must exist (a willingness to pay above the organization’s costs), that competition will occur, and that government guardrails will apply. The claim is simply that the expectations under which the contribution was directed should not be disregarded after the fact.

A third category exists as well: works created and made publicly accessible under expectations formed entirely before, and independent of, any AI system. An example of this is personal content accessible from websites and social media platforms. The rules of the road here are still under construction, and this document will respect those rules as they evolve. That said, there appears to be a common-sense, post-fair-use-epoch framework emerging:

* **Access law:** As long as authorization/access restrictions were not circumvented, scraping is mostly lawful. Arguments about what constitutes circumvention continue.
* **Data protection laws:** For example, EU GDPR holds that public accessibility does not constitute consent. USA has lacked a federally enforced similar privacy law, though numerous states do. There are a truckload of nuances here, and this document does not reflect them all.
* **AI outputs:** There may be further legal evolution in areas such as the outputs of AI models. Increasingly, it appears creators may be able to enforce copyright claims. To date, the developers of AI models have been the targets of lawsuits. However, in theory, AI-tool users are exposed as well.
* **Unsettled:** There are nuances around models that were trained before legal clarification was achieved, what needs to be respected (example robots.txt, Cloudflare blocking, etc). Such issues may be resolved quickly, or not. Regardless of the specifics and nuances that this document does not reflect, the critical principle that appears to be emerging is that public is not consent, and privacy is a concern.

## 2. Core Definitions

* **Common wealth:** The long-term condition in which the typical human experience moves away from precariousness and toward greater security, capability, and voluntary cooperation.
* **Dividends / Rewards:** The returns—economic, reputational, continued opportunity, or free communal use—that the operating model of a system holds out to those who contribute labor, capital, or knowledge.
* **Open systems:** Technical and institutional arrangements whose published terms make free study, use, modification, and redistribution the expected outcome of contribution.
* **Closed systems:** Arrangements whose terms retain proprietary control, accept corresponding costs and liabilities, and promise defined returns to contributors and operators.
* **Ambient works:** Creative and intellectual works made publicly accessible under expectations formed before, and independent of, any AI system—including copyright, attribution norms, and publication terms—whose creators contributed to no AI operating model, open or closed.
* **Coercive extraction:** The compulsory redirection of another party’s labor, capital, data, or compute outside transparent, generally applicable legal processes that themselves respect baseline liberties.
* **Mission model** *(in business, this is referred to as a business model)*: What the individual or organization does, and how it generates rewards for itself and others. For example, an open system may make technology available to all and generate rewards such as community recognition and the diffusion of technology to protect against concentration of access to a technology. A closed system may develop a finished product or service that is proprietary, and generate rewards such as profit, employee compensation, and investor returns.
* **Operating model:** How the business model is developed, for example, organization structure, processes, technology, people, and governance.

## 3. The Structure of AI Contributions

Modern AI rests on open contributions, closed contributions, and ambient works. Because each operates under different expectations, each generates different legitimate claims.

* **Open contributions:** When researchers, developers, or organizations release techniques, architectures, weights, or tools under terms that promise free communal use, the realization of that contribution should be free use. This includes the right to run, study, fine-tune, distill, evaluate, and build upon the released artifacts. Retroactive restriction after voluntary open release violates the expectations under which the potential was directed.
* **Closed contributions:** Large-scale training, reliable inference services, agent frameworks, and the supporting infrastructure require substantial capital and ongoing liability. When contributors and operators are promised financial or proprietary returns under a closed model, those returns should be respected so long as the system generates them. Providers may set and enforce terms of use that protect their investment and manage risk. Protection against unauthorized extraction of the proprietary system itself is a legitimate claim. This does not create a guaranteed right to profit; it respects the terms under which contributions were made, within the ordinary realities of markets, competition, and existing guardrails.
* **Ambient works:** The largest single input to modern AI is neither an open nor a closed contribution: it is the accumulated body of text, images, code, and other works made publicly accessible under expectations that predate AI training. Public accessibility is not a grant of terms. The expectations under which this potential was directed—copyright, license conditions, attribution norms, and publication choices—are the operative terms, and they deserve the same respect this framework extends to open and closed contributions. Three questions must be kept distinct: how a work was acquired, whether learning from it honors or violates the expectations under which it was published, and whether a system’s outputs substitute for the work itself. Acquisition through circumvention, piracy, or breach of stated access terms violates the creator’s expectations regardless of how transformative the subsequent use. Learning from lawfully accessed works presents a genuine conflict between the creator’s expectations and the tradition—long predating AI—that reading, analysis, and the extraction of unprotectable facts and patterns are free. Outputs that substitute for the original work in its own market violate the creator’s expectations, however the training was conducted.

Many real systems contain elements of more than one category. The governing rule remains the expectations under which a given contribution or work was actually directed and offered.

## 4. Hierarchy of Principles

Derived from the foundational premise that expectations tied to an operating model (or to pre-existing publication terms) should be honored unless limited by established political and legal balancing:

1. **Open contributions remain open.**
Techniques and models released under terms that identify free communal use carry a strong presumption of unrestricted study, use, modification, distillation, and redistribution. Narrow exceptions may be justified only for concrete, high-severity harms and must be publicly stated, prospectively applied where possible, and open to challenge.
2. **Closed contributions retain their terms of use and stakeholder contracts.**
Services and systems offered under closed terms may impose reasonable restrictions that protect costs, liabilities, and the returns explicitly or reasonably held out to contributors. These restrictions do not extend to a general power to prevent independent reinvention or open release of comparable capabilities. No right to profit is asserted; the claim is limited to respect for the terms under which contributions were directed, subject to competition and existing guardrails.
3. **Ambient works retain their pre-existing terms.**
Works contributed to no AI system carry the expectations under which they were published. Unlawful acquisition is not cured by transformative use. Training on lawfully accessed works carries a rebuttable presumption of permissibility where the resulting system does not substitute for the works in their own markets; that presumption is defeated by output substitution, by circumvention of stated access terms, or by acquisition from illicit sources. Where training at scale materially erodes the reward structure under which a class of works is created, the burden shifts to the developer to demonstrate that the erosion is attributable to lawful competition rather than to unremunerated use of the works themselves.
4. **National frameworks may limit expectations, subject to specific constraints on AI-specific regulation.**
Political agreements and legal frameworks may constrain open, closed, and ambient expectations when necessary to protect the commonwealth or core national interests. AI-specific regulation is subject to the following considerations:
* Those proposing AI-specific regulation bear the burden of establishing concrete risk to liberties, safety of others, or legitimate public-sector concerns.
* AI remains embryonic; the opportunity for continued improvement and innovation must be weighed in any regulatory design. Specifically, while there are examples of regulation before harm has actually occurred, those examples are rare, and often supported by the community the innovation was created by. In the case of AI, regulators should consider both open and closed systems.
* The risk of regulatory capture must be treated as a first-order concern. Prescriptions should not produce concentrated ownership of, or concentrated access to, AI technology, especially as regulators are the most powerful force in a nation, backed by police and other powerful enforcement mechanisms.
* Where a significant public risk is determined, the resulting prescription must be developed through an open process in which affected stakeholders have both the right and the practical opportunity to contribute.


5. **Self-interest remains a primary driver.**
Individuals, firms, research communities, and nations pursue their own interests. This is a principal engine of contribution. Conflicts are to be resolved through open processes in which affected parties can participate and influence outcomes, not through unilateral override by the currently strongest actor.

## 5. Government Obligations and Costs

Governments are expected to protect both baseline freedoms and the legitimate rewards that flow from open and closed operating models, as well as the pre-existing expectations attached to ambient works. Meeting that obligation imposes costs—regulatory, fiscal, and opportunity costs. Those costs are legitimate only when the conditions in principle 4 are met: the burden of establishing risk has been carried, the embryonic character of the technology and the need for continued innovation have been considered, the risk of creating concentrated ownership or access has been addressed, and significant prescriptions have been developed through open stakeholder processes. AI-specific regimes that impose uniquely heavy burdens without satisfying these conditions fall outside the tradition of balanced national constraint.

Governments are not only regulators, but organizations that use AI. How governments use AI to harm the privacy, liberties, and opportunities of humans, or improve the lives of humans, is a central concern to humans. There must be a proclivity towards openness and transparency when it comes to the use of AI. There have always been and may always be opaqueness when it comes to military use of any technology. The effectiveness of the military is a broadly supported outcome of many humans in a nation. At the same time, there are concerns. Worst case, there must be congressional oversight. Best case, further transparency.

## 6. Inviolate Rights

Certain expectations remain outside ordinary balancing and may not be extinguished by commercial advantage, majority preference, or expansive national-security claims:

* Freedom of expression, association, and participation in the formation of the rules that govern AI.
* The right of individuals and legitimate organizations to defend their persons, systems, and contributions against attack.
* Freedom from coercive extraction of resources outside transparent, generally applicable legal processes that themselves respect these rights.

## 7. Application Notes

* Distillation, fine-tuning, and further development of openly released models fall under principle 1 unless a specific, publicly justified exception applies.
* Unauthorized extraction or reverse-engineering of closed proprietary systems falls under principle 2.
* Training-corpus provenance falls under principle 3: acquisition from pirated or illicit sources, or in breach of stated access terms, violates ambient-work expectations independent of any fair-use analysis of the training itself.
* Model outputs that reproduce or closely substitute for identifiable ambient works fall under principle 3 regardless of the lawfulness of training, and the burden lies on the developer to demonstrate non-substitution.
* The access terms govern distillation from a closed system’s outputs actually accepted (principle 2), not by ambient-work expectations; providers may restrict it by contract but acquire no protection against independent reinvention of comparable capability.
* Export controls, mandatory licensing, compute restrictions, or other AI-specific measures must satisfy the burden-of-risk, innovation, anti-capture, and open-process requirements of principle 4.
* When expectations derived from open, closed, or ambient sources conflict, or when national and transnational interests collide, the burden lies on the party seeking restriction to demonstrate both concrete harm and consistency with the conditions in principle 4.

These principles do not eliminate hard trade-offs. They establish a clear order of presumption rooted in the expectations under which physical and intellectual potential is directed, require that national limitations meet explicit conditions of justification and process, and place the burden of justification on those who would curtail openness, promised returns, or baseline liberties.
